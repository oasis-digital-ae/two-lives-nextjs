The Fear of Slowing Down: Why High Performers Find It So Difficult to Stop

2026-07-02 • By Basim Yafai

The Fear of Slowing Down: Why High Performers Find It So Difficult to Stop

 

It's Rarely About Being Busy

One of the questions I hear most often isn't, "How do I become more successful?"

It's usually something much quieter.

"Why can't I switch off?"

The person asking the question might be an entrepreneur who checks emails before getting out of bed, an athlete who feels guilty taking a recovery day, a CEO whose mind never leaves the business, or a poker player replaying every decision long after the tournament has finished.

On the surface, it looks like dedication.

People admire it. They call it discipline, ambition or commitment.

But after working with high performers, I've come to believe something different.

The fear isn't always slowing down. The fear is what might surface when the noise finally disappears.

 

Success Can Become a Very Good Distraction

There is nothing wrong with working hard.

There is nothing wrong with wanting to build a successful business, compete at the highest level, or lead with ambition. Those things can be incredibly meaningful.

The challenge begins when constant movement becomes the only way you know how to live.

I've met people who can negotiate million-pound deals without hesitation, perform in front of thousands, or make life-changing decisions under pressure. Yet ask them to spend twenty minutes with no phone, no laptop and no agenda, and they become deeply uncomfortable.

Not because they're incapable of resting.

Because they've forgotten what it feels like to simply be, rather than constantly do.

When life becomes a cycle of goals, deadlines and achievements, stillness can start to feel unfamiliar. And when something feels unfamiliar, the mind naturally wants to avoid it.

 

Something I've Noticed

One thing I've noticed after working with entrepreneurs, athletes, leaders and elite competitors is that they often mistake movement for progress.

Being busy feels productive...

Answering another email feels useful...

Planning the next project feels like growth.

Sometimes it is, but sometimes it's simply another way of avoiding the conversations we're not ready to have with ourselves.

Questions like:

  • Am I actually happy?
  • Do I still enjoy what I'm building?
  • Who am I when I'm not performing?
  • Is this still the life I want?

Those questions don't usually appear when you're rushing from one task to another.

They tend to appear in the quiet moments.

Perhaps that's why so many people avoid those moments without even realising it.

 

When the Noise Finally Stops

This is one of the ideas that sits at the heart of Two Lives Theory.

Many high performers spend years becoming exceptional at managing the external world.

Businesses grow.

Careers develop.

Results improve.

But while one part of life continues moving forwards, another part quietly waits for attention.

The internal world.

The thoughts that haven't been explored.

The emotions that have been postponed.

The version of yourself that has changed without you taking the time to notice.

I've often found that the next chapter of growth doesn't begin when someone works harder.

It begins when they finally create enough space to hear themselves again.

That can feel uncomfortable at first.

But discomfort isn't always a sign that something is wrong.

Sometimes it's simply a sign that you've stopped running long enough to notice what needs your attention.

 

Rest Isn't the Opposite of Ambition

One of the biggest misconceptions is that slowing down means becoming less driven.

I don't believe that for a second.

The healthiest high performers I've worked with are still ambitious. They still have goals. They still care deeply about excellence.

The difference is that they no longer confuse constant activity with meaningful progress.

They understand that recovery improves performance.

Reflection improves decision-making... Presence strengthens relationships, and creating space isn't a step backwards. It's often what allows the next step forwards to become clear.

The strongest people aren't always the ones who keep pushing. Sometimes they're the ones who know when it's time to pause.

 

A Question Worth Sitting With

If your calendar suddenly became empty for one week...

No meetings.

No training.

No work.

No notifications.

How would that make you feel?

Relieved? Or uncomfortable?

Your answer might tell you more about where you are in life than any productivity tracker ever could.

 

Final Thoughts

Perhaps slowing down isn't something to fear.

Perhaps it's something to learn.

Because the life you've been working so hard to build deserves more than your constant attention.

It deserves your presence.

Success becomes far more meaningful when you have the capacity to experience it, rather than simply move on to the next goal.

If you've found yourself constantly chasing, constantly thinking, or constantly moving, maybe life isn't asking you to achieve more right now.

Maybe it's inviting you to slow down long enough to hear what your next chapter is trying to tell you.
